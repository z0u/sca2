Why editing block 1 alone removes the op¶

Nothing pulls block 1 under the step recipe, yet editing it alone removes most of the op. The reason is that block 1 writes the evidence for the op onto e₁, and one later block reads it off the example answers. An edit removes the op when it sits between that writer and that reader, and block 1 is upstream of every reader.

Observations¶

  • Where the evidence is written (E1): block 1 is the first place the evidence for the op can be computed, and both recipes write about half of it there, whether or not block 1 is pulled. At block 1 each example answer gathers the colors of its own operands.
  • What the later blocks add (E2): the later blocks mostly carry the alignmentAlignment How far a state points along e₁: the cosine between the state and e₁, which for a unit-length state is its component along e₁; 1 on the anchor direction and 0 off it. forward. Under the edit at block 1 they put back only a little of it, even though the operand colors are still there to compute it from.
  • The reader, and the edits at one slice (E3): on each run one block reads the example answers from the query =, usually block 3. An edit at one slice removes the op when it sits between block 1 and that reader, which accounts for the seeds where the edit at blocks 2 to 4 fell short.

This is an exploratory re-analysis, with no preregistration and no gate. It covers 16 anchored runs, all trained for 400 epochs on the in-context grammar with difference anchored on e₁:

The two controls of ex-2.2.23 at seeds 704 and 710 were trained with no pull and no anti term.

Each block adds to the residual stream twice, first with its attention and then with its MLP. So the figures follow the stream at nine points: the embedding, then after the attention and after the MLP of each of the four blocks. A slice is the stream after a whole block, so slice 1 is the point after the MLP of block 1.

Every pass uses the 14,000 held-out contexts of ex-2.2.21, 2,000 per op. The edit is the full e₁ edit, applied after the named blocks, at every position unless stated. Both recipes write about half of the final alignment in block 1: a little over half of that in its attention, and the rest in its MLP. After block 1, α at the example answers is 0.29 to 0.38 across the 16 runs, 0.5 to 0.7 of its value after block 4. The embedding has none of it, and the controls stay near zero throughout.

The profile through block 1 is about the same under falloff, which does not pull block 1, as under τ_ℓ fixed, which does. So the pull at block 1 itself seems to make little difference to what block 1 writes. The pull at slices 2 to 4 reaches block 1 anyway: the stream carries what block 1 writes to every later slice, so the gradient of the pull there flows back along the residual path to the parameters of block 1. The cheapest way to have e₁ aligned at three later slices is to write it once, at the earliest block that can, and let the stream carry it.

The attention fits this account. Block 1 is the first block where an example answer can look at its operands, and on every run it does: it puts 0.14 to 0.55 of its attention on the two operands of its own example. Blocks 2 and 3 look at them too, on some runs as much, but they come after the alignment has appeared.

The color probe at the answer shows what that attention brings. The operand colors are partly recoverable from the answer even at the embedding, since an answer says something about its operands under most ops. At the attention of block 1 that rises on every run, the controls included, then eases down through the later blocks. How far it rises varies a great deal from run to run, and it does not separate the anchored runs from the controls. So gathering the operands at the answer is something the model does for the task anyway, and the pull puts the evidence for the op, judged from them, onto e₁.

Discussion¶

The residual stream makes "where the pull acts" and "where the concept is written" separate questions. The pull names slices, and training answers with the earliest block that can compute the concept, because one write there serves every pulled slice after it. On this task that is block 1, since the per-example judgement needs one attention step (the answer gathers its operands) and one MLP. The lean of embedding-leanembedding-lean Where the pull reaches the embedding, the color embeddings lean onto e₁ by lightness, a stand-in for the op that the edit then takes from every op. looks like the same effect one step earlier: where the pull reached the embedding, the model answered with the nearest thing the embedding could offer. So keeping the pull off block 1 did not keep the concept off block 1. It kept the stand-ins off it, which was its purpose.

The write bound of edit-effectsedit-effects On falloff, the blocks after the edit put back no more e₁ on the states of the other ops than the clean model has there. already treats the edit at each slice as acting on what arrives there. This account adds that most of what arrives at blocks 2 to 4 on e₁ was put there by block 1. So the edit at every slice does most of its work at slice 1, and the later slices find little left to turn.

It also says why the edit at every slice is the robust choice, as the D2.2 design argues. An edit at fewer slices has to land between the writer and the reader, and the reader forms at a different block from seed to seed. The edit at every slice covers every pairing. A larger model has readers at many depths that we don't choose, so this is the property that would carry over.

The task needs three steps in sequence: each example answer judges itself against its operands, the query = reads those judgements, and the query applies the op it infers. That fits in two blocks. If which block becomes the reader is set by when the reading forms during training, a two-block model would leave it only block 2, and the edit at slice 1 would be the whole edit. That would make a cleaner picture of the mechanism, though without the depth that makes the reader vary.
