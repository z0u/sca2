When the spill appears¶

On three of the four seeds that end over the spill criterion it appears early in training, and on the fourth only late, on an op that run learned late. pool 1–3, soft τ from soft-pool kept the anchor on all eight of its seeds, but only four of its edits stayed within the spill criterion. A cheap early test for spill would let it, or arms tuned from it, run at many seeds, as the loss-rate screen did for losing the anchor. But nothing in the stored trajectories up to epoch 64 predicted the end spill. The stored runs keep only their end checkpoints, so we couldn't tell when the spill appears.

So we trained the condition again at its seeds (704 to 711), with three measurements at every record:11 Record: a point along training where the measurements are taken, every four epochs. the edit itself, a reference edit along another direction as its baseline, and a probe for the colors at the other ops. The reference edit replaces the control as the baseline, since there is no control trajectory to net against. We also scored the edit and the reference edit on the controls, and on the epoch-64 checkpoints the loss-rate screen left.

Observations¶

  • The reference edit beside the control (E1): the reruns match the stored runs. At the end of training, netting the edit against the reference edit sorts the eight seeds much as netting it against the control does. On the controls themselves, the e₁ edit costs some ops a few hundredths more than the reference edit does and others less, so the control adds a seed-to-seed term of its own.   • Spill along training (E2): spill appears in two ways. On three seeds it appears during the rise, as the pool sharpens and the anchor weight reaches its plateau. On 711 it stays to the end, on 707 it settles near the criterion, and on 708 it falls back under it. These three also swing the most from record to record while it lasts. On two seeds it grows after epoch 100, on value-hsv. Around epoch 64 the seeds sort roughly as they do at the end, apart from the late kind; the full order settles at about epoch 180.

On the seeds that learn it early, the edit costs value-hsv less than a reference edit does. The two quiet conditions learn it as late on some seeds and spill little on it.
  • Color on e₁ at the other ops (E4): e₁ holds almost no color information at the other ops, from about epoch 30 on, on every seed, and removing it barely changes how well the color can be read from the rest of the state. The fit from the whole state doesn't follow the spill either.

Scope and method¶

This is an exploratory study, with no preregistration and no gate. It covers one condition, pool 1–3, soft τ, at eight seeds, trained exactly as in soft-pool. That condition pools the pull over slices 1 to 3, and its position and slice pool temperatures sharpen from soft to sharp over the 40-epoch warm-up. Training is deterministic, and the reruns reproduce the stored runs: the end spillSpill The side effect of an edit on the other ops: the largest drop in the score of any op other than the one removed, net of what the same edit does to a control trained without the anchor. The selectivity criterion of D2.2 allows at most 0.02 at any dose. matches to the last digit on every seed (largest difference 0).

Eight seeds is few for correlations: a rank correlation of 0.6 over eight seeds comes up by chance about one time in ten. The seeds also split unevenly. One seed (711) spills far more than the rest, and the other three over the criterion are only just over it. We looked at several measurements, windows and slices after the runs came in, so we take a single strong correlation here as a lead only.

The criterion of 0.02 is a convention, carried over from ex-2.2.16, rather than a line the models draw themselves. On runs of conditions that don't spill, the score scatters by about 0.006 (one standard deviation, over 27 stored runs of such conditions), so the criterion sits about three of those above a quiet run. The seeds close to it (705, 708 and 710) could land on either side of a slightly different criterion or score.

The epoch-64 checkpoints are those of falloff and step, soft τ from the loss-rate screen, 68 in all, and these two conditions spill little. Eleven of the checkpoints belong to runs trained to the end, so their end spill is known, and three of those eleven end at or over the criterion. So the checkpoints can show whether an early edit finds spill that isn't there at the end, and whether it misses spill that is.

The measurements¶

  • Spill, net of control (at the end, as earlier reports score it): the largest drop on another op under the edit, less the drop on that op under the same edit on the control at the same seed, taken over the four doses. The edit projects e₁ out at every position and slice. A run is within the criterion when its spill is at most 0.02. E1 shows that this score carries a seed-to-seed term from the control, since e₁ isn't a typical direction on an unanchored model either, so it is less reliable than it looked.
  • Spill, net of reference (along training): the largest drop on another op under the full edit, less the median drop on that op under eight reference edits. Each reference edit projects out one other basis direction (e₂, e₃, …), chosen at random, and the same eight are used throughout. We use the median because on some seeds one of the eight directions costs every op far more than the rest: Adam gives the residual stream a privileged basis, with the separator tokens on one coordinate that holds about half the stream's energy and = on another, and our draw includes the separator coordinate of three seeds (704, 705 and 708). A label-free rule for later experiments is to draw the reference axes from outside the stream's highest-energy coordinates. Each drop is measured in expected exact matchExpected exact match The task score, as the chance that a drawn answer agrees with a true one. With drawn answers, the correct answer to a line is spread over two or more colors, so we take the chance that an answer drawn from the model agrees with one drawn from the true distribution. Its ceiling is that same chance for the true distribution against itself (Σq²), below 1 on the ops that round. over the whole held-out set, so the noise from the choice of contexts is small (a standard error of about 0.002 per op).
  • Smoothed spill and change per record (along training): the spill net of reference, smoothed with a Gaussian over about 8 epochs either side; and the size of its change from one record to the next, smoothed the same way, which measures how much it swings.
  • When an op is learned: the first epoch at which its task score (without any edit) reaches half its final value. The ops are learned in jumps, and this marks the middle of the jump.
  • The probe: a ridge regression (a linear fit with a small penalty on the weights) from the state at a position to the RGB of the color there. It is fitted on half of the held-out contexts and scored on the other half as \(R^2\) (the share of the variance of the color it predicts). It is fitted at every color position of slices 2 to 4, on the contexts of the other ops and on difference contexts. Each fit uses one of three versions of the state: the whole state, the state without its e₁ coordinate, or the e₁ coordinate alone.

The reference edit beside the control (E1)¶

Can the reference edit stand in for the control as the baseline the spillSpill The side effect of an edit on the other ops: the largest drop in the score of any op other than the one removed, net of what the same edit does to a control trained without the anchor. The selectivity criterion of D2.2 allows at most 0.02 at any dose. is netted against? At the end of training we have both, so the figure (left) and the table compare them on the eight reruns. We also apply the edit and the reference edit to the controls and to the epoch-64 checkpoints, where the edit should have little to act on (right).

We first tried a random direction orthogonal to e₁ as the reference edit, as planned. On a control it cost every op far more than the e₁ edit does, so a random direction is no baseline for e₁. A single other basis direction behaves much more like e₁ on a control, though, so we used eight of those as the reference edits. The gap comes from the few high-energy coordinates: a random direction touches all of them, and a random direction kept off them costs about what an ordinary axis does.

The two scores rank the eight seeds alike (rank correlation 0.90), though they often name different ops. The reference-netted score runs a little lower, so the criterion would need restating for it. The seeds over the criterion stay at or over it, apart from 708, which falls under it.

On the controls, the e₁ edit costs some ops up to a few hundredths more than the reference edit does, and others as much less, differently on each seed (the table shows only the largest). So e₁ on an unanchored model isn't quite a typical direction, and netting against the control at the same seed adds that seed-to-seed term to the spill score. That may be part of why quiet runs scored as high as 0.018 in earlier reports.

On the epoch-64 checkpoints of the two low-spill conditions, the edit costs the other ops a little less than the reference edit, as it should when the anti term keeps them off e₁. Only 1 of the 68 goes over the criterion, and on the eleven with a known end spill the early score stays below 0.01. So an early edit doesn't seem to invent spill. But it also misses the spill that three of the eleven end with (at or over the criterion). That is the late kind of spill from E2, seen here in two other conditions.

Spill along training (E2)¶

When does the spillSpill The side effect of an edit on the other ops: the largest drop in the score of any op other than the one removed, net of what the same edit does to a control trained without the anchor. The selectivity criterion of D2.2 allows at most 0.02 at any dose. appear, and from when does it sort the seeds as the end does? The figure shows the reference-netted spill smoothed over neighboring records, how much it swings from one record to the next, and how well each of the two sorts the seeds as the end does. The table gives the spill averaged over a few windows.

Spill appears in two ways. The early kind rises on seeds 708 and 711 between epochs 24 and 32, as the pool temperature reaches its sharp value and the anchor weight its plateau, and on 707 at about epoch 44, just after. It falls mostly on darken and hue-hsv, and it stays up past where the loss-rate screen stopped. After that the three seeds differ. On 711 it dips around epoch 160 and climbs again to the end, on darken. On 707 it moves to mix and settles near the criterion, and on 708 it falls back to about half.

The late kind grows on seeds 705 and 710, from about epoch 120 and 150 respectively, on value-hsv, while the anti weight on slice 3 is partway through its slow anneal. It ends just under the criterion on 705 and just over it on 710. E3 looks at why these two seeds.

The early kind is also the jumpy kind. During the rise (epochs 20 to 180) the spill of 707, 708 and 711 changes by 0.016 per record on average, against 0.006 on the other five seeds, including 705 and 710, whose late spill grows smoothly. After epoch 200 every seed settles (a standard deviation of about 0.003 after epoch 300). So the swings flag the same runs as the level of the early spill does, and nothing more.

The seeds within the criterion settle a little below zero (704, 706 and 709, at about −0.01): there the edit costs every other op a little less than a typical other axis does. That is how a very clean anchor looks, with the other ops leaving e₁ alone while making some use of most other coordinates, though a score a little above zero is still within the criterion.

Smoothing doesn't bring the order forward much. The smoothed spill sorts the seeds as the end does from about epoch 180 on (a rank correlation over 0.9), and before about epoch 130 its rank correlation hovers around 0.5 to 0.6. Averaged over epochs 48 to 80, 707, 708 and 711 are clearly up, 710 is lowest of all, and 705 is among the runs within the criterion. The window over epochs 100 to 160 does better by rank (0.81) because the late rise of 705 and 710 has begun by then, but its correlation by value is 0.67. The correlations by value lean on 711 early on: without it, the window over epochs 24 to 40 correlates 0.30 with the end.

So a screen at epoch 64 would catch the early kind of spill and miss the late kind. On these seeds it would catch three of the four runs over the criterion, though two of those three end near it.

The late kind and learning value-hsv (E3)¶

Is the late kind tied to when the run learns value-hsv? Each op is learned in a jump, at a different time on each seed. The selectivity criterion of D2.2 allows at most 0.02 at any dose. on value-hsv beside its task score.

The seeds learn value-hsv between about epoch 60 and 128. The two seeds with the late kind are among the last to learn it (705 at epoch 108, 710 at 128, the latest), and their spill on it grows while they learn it and then stays. 708, which learns it at 100, ends with a little spill on it too (0.008). On the seeds that learn it early, the edit costs value-hsv less than a reference edit does, by 0.015 to 0.05 at the end, so there value-hsv keeps further off e₁ than off a typical axis. Over the eight seeds, the later a seed learns value-hsv, the more the edit costs it at the end (rank correlation 0.71). 709 is the exception: it learns value-hsv late (epoch 116) and still keeps it off e₁.

When an op is learned isn't fixed by the seed, though (table). The control also learns value-hsv at different times on different seeds, between epochs 48 and 144, but which seeds are late changes with the condition, and in this condition the order is unrelated to the control's. So the anchor and anti terms shift when the op is learned. The two quiet conditions learn it as late or later on some seeds, step, soft τ at 180 on 709 and falloff at 164 on 706, and spill little on it. Learning late doesn't put an op on e₁ by itself; in those conditions something keeps e₁ closed to it.

The early kind fits the same pattern loosely. Every seed learns darken by epoch 28, during the warm-up, and the early spill of 708 and 711 falls on it. On 707 the early spill falls on hue-hsv, which 707 learns at epoch 48, earlier than any other seed.

So, as a lead: in this condition the spill lands on an op that is being learned at a time when e₁ is open to it, during the rise or later, and the quiet conditions keep e₁ closed to an op they learn as late. Within this condition, it doesn't say which way it runs. Learning value-hsv late may leave it free to land on e₁, or whatever draws it to e₁ may also slow its learning.

Color on e₁ at the other ops (E4)¶

Does e₁ hold color information at the example answers of the other ops, which the edit would take away, and does that track the spillSpill The side effect of an edit on the other ops: the largest drop in the score of any op other than the one removed, net of what the same edit does to a control trained without the anchor. The selectivity criterion of D2.2 allows at most 0.02 at any dose.? This is a geometric measurement that doesn't apply the edit. The figure shows the probe at the example answers, at slice 3.

Removing e₁ barely changes how well the color can be read from the state: the largest difference between the whole state and the state without e₁ is 0.016 in \(R^2\), at any position, slice or record, on the other ops and on difference alike. The color is spread over many coordinates, so one coordinate more or less makes little difference to a linear readout.

On its own, e₁ holds little color at the example answers of the other ops. After the first few epochs its \(R^2\) is about 0.01, with the seeds that spill and those that don't intermixed (rank correlation with the end spill −0.43 at epoch 64). On difference contexts it holds more, about 0.02, which is still little. What the anchor puts on e₁ there is the op, difference, rather than a color, so there is no reason for e₁ to carry much of the RGB.

The whole-state fit on the other ops falls slowly over training, and on two seeds it falls further: from the start on 711, which spills most, and after about epoch 60 on 706, which doesn't spill. So the fit doesn't follow the spill (rank correlation −0.43 at epoch 64, −0.07 at the end).

So the probe doesn't see the spill. The edit takes away e₁, but what the other ops lose may be how later blocks read e₁, rather than color information stored there.

Discussion¶

The edit spillsSpill The side effect of an edit on the other ops: the largest drop in the score of any op other than the one removed, net of what the same edit does to a control trained without the anchor. The selectivity criterion of D2.2 allows at most 0.02 at any dose. in two ways in this condition. The early kind appears during the rise, when the pool is still sharpening and the anchor weight reaches its plateau. That fits the reading of soft-pool that the soft pool lifts the other ops toward e₁ at that time. The late kind appears on value-hsv on two of the seeds that learn it last, while they learn it, as the anti term is partway through its slow anneal (E3). On 705 it starts while the anti weight is still near 0.2, so the anneal may matter less than when the op is learned. Both kinds land on an op that is being learned at the time. Neither reading is tested here; each would need an arm that changes the schedule at that time.

A screen that edits a checkpoint around epoch 64 would see only the early kind (E2). On these seeds the late kind was the smaller, but it put one run over the criterion and another just under it, so such a screen would undercount. A screen late enough to see both, around epoch 200, would cost half a full run.

Pooled over seeds, epoch 64 does tell the three conditions apart (table). pool 1–3, soft τ averages 0.014 at epoch 64, and half its runs end over the criterion. falloff and step, soft τ average about −0.005, and few of their runs end over it. So a condition whose runs often spill early shows it in the mean at epoch 64, even when a single run is noisy. But the mean can't see the late kind, and the two quiet conditions look alike at epoch 64 though they differ a little at the end. With three conditions this is a hint only: a pooled early score could rank arms by their early kind of spill, with a later check still needed for the late kind. The swings during the rise could serve the same way, since they mark the same runs.

Is that enough to stop a condition early? As a rule for dropping a condition whose runs spill early, it may be. Scoring a record costs about 1% of training, and the runs of a condition that passes simply carry on, so the screen costs almost nothing, and each condition it stops saves the remaining five-sixths of its runs. It can't stop a condition for the late kind, so a condition that passes still needs its full runs before it is adopted. It pays when many arms are screened and a fair share of them spill early. With few arms, or arms that spill late, it saves little.

Netting against a reference edit works about as well as netting against a control at the end of training, and it needs no control run. The controls showed that e₁ isn't a neutral direction on an unanchored model either, so some of the noise in the earlier spill scores may come from the control rather than from the anchored run.
