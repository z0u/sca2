Sparse Concept Anchoring in transformers¶

Sparse Concept Anchoring (SCA) is a training-time method for concept control. A light geometric regularizer, driven by a small number of noisy labels, guides a chosen concept toward a known location in representation space. The geometry is shaped during training, so there is no need to reverse-engineer it afterwards: the concept lives where you put it, and the side effects of suppressing or ablating it can be bounded before running the intervention.

The first milestone (M1) established the method in autoencoders: paper, blog post, code. This site holds the experiment reports for the second milestone (M2), which asks: does SCA transfer to transformers? We train a small transformer to do vector math and anchor concepts in its residual stream. The vectors are colors, in a synthetic color-mixing task (red + blue = purple) where ground truth is unambiguous, so a negative result stays interpretable. The plan and deliverables are in the project README.

D2.1: anchoring in a transformer¶

D2.1 asked whether SCA works in a transformer at all. We designed a task where a small transformer learns the geometry of color on its own, then anchored red to a chosen direction in its residual stream. With a repulsive term, a schedule for it, and a pull that finds the red operand by itself, red landed where we put it, graded by how red each color is, at no measurable cost to the task.

Reports

  • 2.1.1. Un-anchored color-mixing transformer

    A small transformer learns a character-level language of color-mixing equations, solving the forms it saw in training and unseen hex pairs. Color turns out to be linearly decodable from its residual stream, with each seed putting redness in a different place. Held-out named pairs sit at zero accuracy.

    The previous model never answered a held-out named pair, so we changed the grammar to make composition the only route: reverse alias lines, and named equations whose mix falls off the palette. The model picks up both new skills and still won't chain them within one forward pass, leaving held-out named accuracy at zero.

    No hex codes, just one opaque token per color and nothing in the text to say that colors are values at all. The model infers the geometry from co-occurrence alone: its embeddings hold the RGB cube as a linear subspace, it computes mixes in value space just before answering, and its held-out guesses land on or beside the right color.

    Does geometry inference need one token per concept? Not at 216 colors. We test the same equations but with every color spelled as an opaque four-letter name. There's a small drop in held-out accuracy, but the value subspace survives. Reading names takes most of the network's depth, leaving one layer for the mix to live in. The 27-color grid is too coarse.

D2.2: anchoring an operation¶

D2.2 anchors an operation rather than a color. Since the pivot, the model infers which op a line uses from a few solved examples, so the op has no word of its own, like the abstract concepts we care about in language models. The anchor lands at little cost to the task, and removing the anchor direction takes the op out gradually. Under the edit, the model moves its answers onto the other ops about as much as an ideal predictor would, though context by context it doesn't always pick the same ones. On the recipe of record, which pools the pull over blocks 1 to 3, the anchor held on every run at fresh seeds, the edit removes most of the op, and the other ops stay within the selectivity criterion on every seed. A model trained without the op lands only a little closer to the target nullTarget null Where a removal should land: what an ideal predictor that had lost one op and nothing else would answer. It is the Bayes posterior over ops given the examples, with the removed op set to zero and the rest renormalized, so it still weighs the other ops by how well they fit. than the edited model, so most of the distance left is what a model of this size does on contexts like these. Removing red¶

D2.1 anchored red but never removed it, so D2.2 began by removing it. Projecting out the anchor direction took red out, and took out more where the line was redder, at a small cost to lines with no red. Of the edits we tried, the plain projection stayed the best choice.

A richer grammar for red¶

Before we could anchor an op, the grammar needed more than one, so it grew from one op to eleven. We anchored red again at each step, and the recipe held. Removal was clean on every op but one, where the model kept about a quarter of the answers that needed red.

Anchoring an op word, and the pivot¶

Ex-2.2.14 anchored the op difference, and it landed, but almost entirely on the word difference, so removing it would be hard to tell apart from deleting the word. The concepts we care about in language models mostly have no word of their own, so we took the op words out: each line now shows a few solved examples and a query, and the model has to infer the op.

Teaching the in-context task¶

Once the op had no word, the first question was whether an un-anchored model could learn the task at all. At first it got a little under halfway from guessing to the best possible score. Changes to training and a smaller set of ops brought it to about nine tenths of the way.

Anchoring and removing the inferred op¶

With the control near its best, we anchored difference on the new grammar. The anchor settles on the example answers, where the context shows the most about the op. Removing the anchor direction everywhere takes difference out gradually. At first the edit spilled onto the other ops on most fully trained runs. The spillSpill The side effect of an edit on the other ops: the largest drop in the score of any op other than the one removed, net of what the same edit does to a control trained without the anchor. The selectivity criterion of D2.2 allows at most 0.02 at any dose. came from a stand-in for the op in the color embedding table, where there is no context yet to infer it from. With the pull kept off the first two slices and the anti-subspace weight held high there, the edit removes difference and leaves the other ops within the criterion on every fresh seed. That recipe, falloff, was the recipe of record until late October.

What the edit does, and how we measure it¶

With falloff in hand, we looked at what the edit does beyond removal and spill, and at the baseline the edit is measured against. The edit stays within the bound the geometry sets, and lands about half of the way to the target null. The anchor does not rely on e₁ being a coordinate axis, but the comparison with the control did, so the baseline is now the median over the other axes of the run.

A recipe that keeps the anchor on every run¶

falloff lost the anchor on about one run in forty, and its close variants on more. A pull pooled over blocks 1 to 3, with a temperature that starts soft, kept the anchor on every run but spilled more. Sharpening the pool over slices took the spill away, and that recipe, τ_ℓ fixed, kept the anchor on every run at fresh seeds and is now the recipe of record.

Reports

  • An anchor weight that ramps with depth

    A pull weight that rises in a straight line with depth, in place of the step at slice 2, to mirror the falloff of the anti weight. With the slice-4 weight of the step, the ramps lost the anchor on two or three of eight seeds, where the step lost none.
