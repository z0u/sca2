# Design checks

Checks a plan should pass before it runs. The drafter applies them while filling in the template, and the assumptions review applies them again with fresh eyes. Each opens with the practice to follow; the example after it is a time we didn't, and what it cost.

- Choose a measurement by a criterion independent of the statistic you're judging.
- Score something the treatment does not touch by construction (the task metric, a held-out probe, a downstream selectivity), and keep the quantity it optimizes as a manipulation check. A gate, or any comparison an observation rests on, is informative only if a reader cannot predict its direction from the method section alone. Ask what the treatment optimizes, and whether the scored statistic is that quantity, a monotone function of it, or independent of it; only the third tests the mechanism. Example of the pitfall: H4(a) of ex-2.1.7 scored the containment of ᾱ under a term _defined_ as a penalty on ᾱ, so "the term lowers ᾱ" could only ever say the weight was large enough. It passed preregistration and a review round before anyone noticed.
- List everything a factor changes before writing its hypothesis, and look hardest at normalizers, denominators, and anything averaged over a set whose size the factor changes. To test for a side effect, rename the factor after it and see whether the hypothesis still reads as written. If not, either remove the side effect (normalize by a fixed count) or report the matching invariants for every arm, as with bracketing below. Example of the pitfall: the op1-only arm of ex-2.1.7 was read as a pure narrowing of the pull, but the anchor term divides by the realized mask, so one position instead of four also made each pull about 3.9× stronger. The reading survived only because the ceiling arm happened to separate the two.
- Ablate before you search, and bracket rather than survey when the ablation needs a value you haven't found yet. Removing a schedule means running a constant instead, and which constant you pick can decide the answer. So rather than matching on one invariant (area, maximum, or endpoint, each defensible and each a different condition), choose flat levels that straddle the range of the schedule. If the schedule beats every constant in its range, no constant substitutes for the shape, whatever the optimum turns out to be; if one wins, the schedule dimensions go away and you have a better operating point too. Report the matching invariants for every arm, so the results can say which was the active ingredient. Each dimension deleted this way is much cheaper than searching it.
- Compare conditions as two samples: the difference of seed means with a Welch t interval, and Fisher's exact test for rates. At eight runs a side a percentile bootstrap covers about 90% of the time where it claims 95% (*fixed-effects*), so prefer the t interval there. Pair runs by seed only when they branch from one stored state and are measured soon after, and size the seed count from the seed spread (at eight seeds per condition, removal gaps under about 0.05 are hard to see). A change of 0.6% in the slice temperature left the two runs of a seed up to 0.43 apart in removal by epoch 32, and by epoch 400 removal under the two recipes correlated about 0.2 across seeds (*fixed-confirm*); a seed-by-seed reading there would have found a story for each seed. The reasoning is in [eng/determinism.md](/eng/determinism.md#seeds-are-draws).
- Show the cards each condition ran on. Training roles fall back from the L40S to the L4 or A10G when L40Ss are taken, so the mix of cards depends on when a condition launched. Give the report a small table of runs per card per condition (from `gpu` in the training results), and where a comparison is close, check that it holds on the runs of one card alone. Arms compared in pairs from one stored state stay on one card. The reasoning is in [eng/determinism.md](/eng/determinism.md#seeds-are-draws).
