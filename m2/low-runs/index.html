Scope and method¶

This is an exploratory re-analysis of 48 stored runs, with no preregistration and no gate. They are the 24 fixed-label runs of the fixed-labelsfixed-labels With one label draw held for the run, the anchor settles at every rate, but a few runs in eight end well below the rest in removal, and which runs do so moves when a run is trained again with slightly different arithmetic. experiment (three rates at seeds 712 to 719), its 16 crossed runs (the 1/50 sets of four seeds, each trained at all four), and the eight per-visit runs of the fixed confirmation. All use the recipe of record, τ_ℓ fixed, trained for 400 epochs.

Four of the crossed runs pair a label set with its own seed. Each repeats a fixed-labels run from the same initialization, on the same batches, with the same labels. The one difference is the card: the fixed-labels runs trained on L4s and the crossed runs on L40S cards. XLA, the compiler that turns the model into GPU code, compiles a different program for each card model, and a sum taken in a different order differs in its last bits. So the two runs of a pair see the same gradients to within rounding, and part by a little on the first step. We call them twins. Two of the four pairs ended with one run low and one not.

Restricted edits (one slice, or one set of positions) are scored without the median-over-axes baseline, which would need 63 more passes per edit. The baseline takes about a tenth off the full edit in every run. So a restricted edit can be compared with the full edit scored the same way, and only loosely with removal. The slide and peak of E4 use the kept checkpoints of the fixed-labels experiment and the fixed confirmation, scored there.

How can the last bits of the arithmetic decide this? Twins part early and keep parting, so they are two different runs rather than one run with a small change at the end. The distance figure measures how far apart the weights of a pair are, as a share of their size. On that scale, two unrelated runs sit at √2 ≈ 1.41, and the fixed-labels runs at different seeds sit at 1.40 (1.40 to 1.41) at every checkpoint. By epoch 16 the twins are already 0.90 (0.81 to 1.00) apart, most of the way to unrelated, and the gap keeps widening to 1.29 (1.26 to 1.33) at epoch 400.

So by the end, a twin pair is nearly as far apart in weight space as two seeds, while its loss and α agree to a hundredth. The pairs that split are no further apart than the pairs that did not. Twins are different runs that share a function, so a split needs no rare tipping point for a last bit to cross. Which side of the line each run lands on is drawn afresh, with odds that these seeds put near even. Training is known to be sensitive to any perturbation at all. What is new here is which quantity the perturbation changes.

What survives the edit (E2)¶

Which contexts does the edited model still get right, and what does the model know about difference that is not on e₁? In the block1-editblock1-edit Block 1 writes the evidence for the op onto e₁ at the example answers, one later block reads it from the query = through attention, and an edit removes the op when it sits between the two. account, block 1 writes the evidence that an example fits difference onto e₁ at the answer, and a later block reads it. The edit removes the op by clearing that coordinate. If the evidence also had a part off the axis, the reader could take it from there, and the edit would miss that part.

After the full edit, the expected match rises in every run with how many of the three examples fit difference, and far more steeply in the low runs. On contexts where all three fit, the edited low runs still score 0.32 (0.20 to 0.45) against 0.06 (0.02 to 0.14) for the rest. On the noisier contexts both groups are near the floor. So what the edit misses comes from the examples that fit.

The evidence is also written off e₁, in every run. With the e₁ coordinate zeroed, a linear probe on the state at an example answer at slice 3 tells a fitting example from one that does not fit at AUC 0.98 or above in all 48 runs. That is about as well as the e₁ coordinate alone does (0.99 (0.97 to 1.00)). The low runs sit among the rest on both. The op at the query = is off-axis too, at AUC 0.88 (0.65 to 0.94), as block1-edit found: the anchor never held it there.

How many directions does the off-axis evidence take? Few. Here the probe is given only the top principal components of the off-axis state. At slice 2 it needs a median of 2 components to reach AUC 0.95, though a handful of runs need 8 or more, and one never gets there. At slice 3, one component is enough in 46 of 48 runs. A single fixed direction, the one between the mean states of fitting and non-fitting examples, scores 0.99 (0.94 to 1.00) at slice 2 and 0.99 (0.95 to 1.00) at slice 3. By squared length, that mean difference lies 0.75 (0.64 to 0.82) along e₁ at slice 2 and 0.58 (0.53 to 0.66) at slice 3, with the rest along one off-axis direction.

What is that off-axis direction? Two checks say what it is and that it is real. First, the states are unit vectors, so a large e₁ coordinate leaves less length for everything else, and a probe on the zeroed state could be reading that shadow of e₁ rather than a direction. Renormalizing the zeroed states removes the shadow. The direction between the renormalized class means still scores AUC 0.98 (0.94 to 1.00) at slice 3, where the reader reads, and 0.93 (0.78 to 0.98) at slice 2, so the off-axis evidence is a direction the state has. Second, along that direction a non-fitting example sits at −0.86 (−0.93 to −0.75) and a fitting one at −0.21 (−0.35 to −0.08) at slice 3: the direction is the one an answer state has when its example does not fit, and a fitting example has moved away from it, toward e₁. Among the fitting examples the e₁ coordinate and this coordinate move together (r = 0.66 (0.48 to 0.86)), so examples do not divide into some that fit in one respect, written on e₁, and others that fit in another, written elsewhere. They lie along one arc, from the non-fitting state to e₁, and differ in how far along it they sit.

The figure draws that arc for three runs at seed 715: the low run, its twin, and the per-visit run. Each answer state is a unit vector, so in the plane of e₁ and the non-fitting direction a state that lies in the plane sits on the unit circle, and one that sits inside the circle has part of its length in other directions. The non-fitting examples gather on the right, where e₁ is near zero: on the circle at slice 3, and inside it at slice 2. The plane is a projection, so "arc" is a simplification: two non-fitting examples that land on the same point here can be far apart in the other directions, as the ones at slice 2 are, and the figure says nothing about whether an example that fits lighten and one that fits mix sit near each other. What it does say is that every fitting example has left the non-fitting direction by about as much as it has gained on e₁. The fitting examples spread up the circle toward e₁ = 1, further along at slice 3 than at slice 2, and at slice 3 the ones near the top sit at αα Alignment with e₁: the e₁ component of the unit-norm state at the example answers of the held-out difference contexts, at slice 3. close to 1. The three runs have the same shape; the fitting examples of the low run sit a little further from e₁ at slice 3, which is the small α gap in the table of E1. So the writer gives the readers much the same picture in a low run, its twin, and a per-visit run, which is the point of E1 seen from inside.

A model trained without the anchor holds the same arc, found after training from the mean states of the two kinds of example, but a short one: in the eight controls at seeds 712 to 719 the two means lie a median of 21° apart at slice 3 (9° to 35°), where the per-visit runs put them 83° apart (arc-in-controls probe). So the anchor stretches the arc and names one end of it in advance, which makes the arc quicker to read; it is there to be found without it.

The figure above draws the arc that way, from the class means alone, for a control and the per-visit run at seed 715. In the control the fitting examples sit in one tight group just off the non-fitting group, so a direction fitted after training finds both ends. In the per-visit run they spread along a quarter circle, and the end the plane finds from the means is e₁ itself (cosine 0.99 (0.98 to 1.00)), where in the controls the same axis lies nearly at right angles to e₁ (0.04 (−0.19 to 0.24)).

So, to a first approximation, the evidence that an example fits is one vector with two parts. One component lies along e₁, where the anchor placed it. The other is how far the state has left the direction a non-fitting answer has, which the loss made on its own and the anchor never touched. Both parts are there in every run, low or not, and nothing in the end state of the writer (block 1) sets the low runs apart. What has to differ is how much the readers take from each part.

Where the edit misses (E3)¶

Where does the reader take the rest of the evidence from in a low run? The edits here change the full edit in two ways. Some narrow it to one slice. Others go further than e₁ at one set of positions after block 2. One of these removes every direction but e₁ at the example answers, so the next block sees the answers through e₁ alone. The other two apply the full edit and then remove the whole state at the answers or at the other example positions, so whatever the full edit left there is gone too. Where that last kind lifts a low run toward the normal runs under the same edit, the leftover was reaching the reader from those positions. Clearing a whole state is a diagnostic, too costly to use as an intervention: it costs the other ops up to 0.63 in expected match, against 0.10 at most under the full edit.

Before any baseline, the full edit misses more in the low runs. It takes 0.77 (0.68 to 0.87) of the way against 0.95 (0.90 to 0.98) in the rest, so the baseline is not what sets the groups apart. Removing e₁ at the three example answers alone removes as much as the full edit in every run, to within 0.02. So the e₁ that the full edit takes off the other positions makes no difference. No single slice accounts for the gap either. In most runs, low or not, the e₁ edit at slice 2 alone removes nearly as much as the full edit. A few runs, all at seed 712, read at block 2 instead, and the edit at slice 1 removes the op in them.

The low runs come in two shapes. In 5 of the 10, the e₁ edit at slice 3 alone also removes much of the op, so block 4 reads it from the answers as well. Keeping only e₁ at the answers after block 2 costs these runs 0.67 (0.44 to 0.85) of the way, so that reader uses the off-axis content of the answer states. The other 5 look like normal runs in where the op is read (slice 2, block 3) and under the all-but-e₁ edit, which costs them nothing: block 3 takes e₁ when it is there. In the twins, clearing the answers on top of the full edit brings the low run at seed 715 level with its twin, and clearing the other example positions does not. At seed 719 it is the other way round. Across the ten low runs, one clearing or the other lifts the full edit by a tenth or more in 7 (5 by the answers, 5 by the other positions, and 3 by both). In the normal runs neither clearing lifts it by more than 0.08. The 3 low runs that neither clearing lifts are the 3 with the smallest shortfall. So the leftover sits in the off-axis content of the example positions, at the answers or at the others depending on the run. The e₁ edit leaves it because it was never on e₁.

A reader after block 3 does not make a run low on its own: 2 normal runs have one too, and remove the op well. What the low runs share is that their readers take enough of the evidence from off the axis for the e₁ edit to leave about a fifth of the op in place.

Discussion¶

As far as the loss and the anchor term can see, a low run and its twin are the same solution. They have the same loss and the same alignmentAlignment How far a state points along e₁: the cosine between the state and e₁, which for a unit-length state is its component along e₁; 1 on the anchor direction and 0 off it., and a different outcome under the edit. The difference is inside the reader blocks. The evidence that an example fits difference is one vector with two parts: a part along e₁, which the anchor put there, and a part along one other direction, how far the state has left the direction a non-fitting answer has, which the loss made on its own. A reader can look for difference-ness along e₁ or for the absence of not-difference-ness along the other direction, and the readers of the low runs do more of the second. These edits cannot tell whether that is a different weighting of the same two inputs or a reader built a little differently. Either way, it is something we are not optimizing for, and the model has to settle it one way or the other.

The anchor term asks for the e₁ part, and the anti term keeps e₁ off the other positions. But no term asks the readers to use e₁, so training is flat along that choice. A run settles somewhere on it early, as the twins show, and then stays or drifts during the anneal. That fits the fixed-labelsfixed-labels With one label draw held for the run, the anchor settles at every rate, but a few runs in eight end well below the rest in removal, and which runs do so moves when a run is trained again with slightly different arithmetic. finding that neither the label set nor the seed decides which runs end low.

None of this is special to the fixed-label runs. The off-axis evidence is there in the per-visit runs too, and their readers also take some of it: the full edit leaves a few hundredths of the way in every run. The fixed-label runs lean on it more often, and more heavily. The per-visit draw may spread less because the pull reaches nearly every difference context over a run. The e₁ part is then written on the whole corpus, and the readers have less reason to prefer the other part. A fixed set leaves most contexts with only the off-axis part during training. That is a guess, and this re-analysis does not test it.

If it holds, the lever is on the readers rather than on the writer. The most targeted term would make the prediction depend on e₁. On the labelled contexts, it would run a second forward pass with e₁ removed, and train it toward the answer that the absence of the op would give (the target nullTarget null Where a removal should land: what an ideal predictor that had lost one op and nothing else would answer. It is the Bayes posterior over ops given the examples, with the removed op set to zero and the rest renormalized, so it still weighs the other ops by how well they fit.). A run that reads from off the axis would then pay for it. That costs one forward pass per labelled context, or less: the edit can be applied to a random fraction of the labelled contexts inside the ordinary forward pass, with the target null as their target, so a context and its edited copy can sit in one batch. Either way it needs nothing but the labels, so it would carry over to real text. It would need its own experiment. Another option is a second anchored direction for the off-axis part, since the evidence has about two directions. But an earlier multi-axis anchor did not pay off, and the reader could still prefer a third direction.

As for scoring, the low state is invisible to the signals that the loss-rate screen uses, so an early call cannot catch it. The e₁ edit cannot catch it either until about half-way through training (epoch 224 on these runs), since some normal runs start late and sit under the low ones before then.
