A baseline from the quiet axes¶

We re-scored the stored runs of the recipe of record against a baseline that does not depend on it: the median drop over edits along the other quiet coordinate axes. The numbers quoted so far barely move, and on the rotated-basis runs the new baseline brings the short seeds back up beside the rest.

The question comes from rotated-basis, where Adam acted in a rotated basis.11 Adam and the basis: Adam scales the update of each coordinate separately, so the coordinate axes are special to it. Rotated-basis ran it in a rotated basis instead. On two seeds e₁ overlapped the syntax direction of the control, so the edit took half of the op from the control as well. Removal net of the control then fell short, although the anchored runs removed the op as before.

That report and the D2.2 design proposed a different baseline: the median over reference edits along other coordinate axes, leaving out the syntax axes, as in the spill-timing experiment.

This report trains nothing. It scores stored runs: the eight falloff runs (the recipe of record) of split-anti-seeds, the eight controls of ex-2.2.23 at the same seeds, the sixteen runs of rotated-basis, and, for E4, the sixteen runs of two soft-pool arms. On each run it scores the edit along each of the 64 coordinate axes, at every dose.

  • Net removal and spill against the new baseline (E2): little changes. The mean removal rises by a hundredth or two, mostly on the one seed where e₁ was a busy axis of the control. Scope and method¶

This is an exploratory re-scoring, with no preregistration and no gate. The model seeds are 704 to 711, and the controls are the 400-epoch ones. Each anchored run pairs with the control at its seed, which shares its initialization, batches, and label draws.

The edit is the dose edit of ex-2.2.22, unchanged. It projects a fixed direction out of the state at every position and slice, removing a quarter, a half, three quarters, or all of the component along it. It is scored on the whole held-out set of every op, as before.

Along e₁ the drops this edit causes reproduce the published dose scoring to rounding. New here is the same edit along every other coordinate axis, and, for the rotated-basis runs, along every axis of the rotated basis Adam acted in.

A reference axis is any coordinate axis other than e₁ and the two that hold the most energy22 Energy: the sum of the squared projections of the states onto a direction. The share of a direction is its energy over the total. of the stream, averaged over slices. Those two are where the model puts its syntax tokens. For the rotated-basis runs the reference axes are the rotated axes, with the two highest in energy left out the same way.

One coordinate holds about 0.4 of the energy in every control, and E1 shows what projecting it out costs.

Measurements¶

It is the Bayes posterior over ops given the examples, with the removed op set to zero and the rest renormalized, so it still weighs the other ops by how well they fit.. Higher is better.
  • SpillSpill The side effect of an edit on the other ops: the largest drop in the score of any op other than the one removed, net of what the same edit does to a control trained without the anchor. Lower is better.
  • The cost of an axis: the drop under the full edit along it, averaged over the ops. A quiet axis is one that costs little.

Each of these is net of a baseline. We compare three, each taken per op and per dose:

  • e₁ in the control: the drop the same e₁ edit causes in the control at the same seed. This is the measurement quoted so far.
  • Reference axes, control: the median drop over the reference axes of the control.
  • Reference axes, own run: the median drop over the reference axes of the anchored run itself. It needs no control.

E2 also shows removal with nothing subtracted (raw), as rotated-basis recommended.

Where e₁ sits in the control (E1)¶

How typical an axis is e₁ in a control? If it is always among the quiet ones, the paired baseline and the median of the quiet axes should agree, and nothing quoted so far would move. If its place varies by seed, so does the paired baseline.

What we saw. Its place varies. On two seeds e₁ is the second quietest axis of the control. At seed 705 it is the 5th costliest of 64 (the high square in the figure below), with about twice the cost of the median reference axis. The median itself is steady across seeds.

So the paired baseline is one draw from the axes of the control, and that draw ranges from one of the quietest axes to one of the busiest few.

The axis with the most energy takes most of the score of every op on every seed, and all of it on 707 and 708.

The second (the lower open circle) costs as much on most seeds, but on 707 and 708 it is a milder axis, and on 706 two reference axes cost more than it does. So ranking by energy only roughly finds the costly axes.

Leaving the syntax axes out of the reference set hardly matters: the median over all 63 axes other than e₁ gives a removal within 0.005 of the median without them, on every seed. A median of a few dozen axes is not swayed by two.

On 3 of 8 seeds (706, 707, 708) the falloff run puts its most energetic axis (the spike) on a different coordinate from the control at the same seed, at every slice, and that coordinate holds almost nothing in the control. Rotated-basis expected otherwise: it found the spike on the same coordinate in both runs on the seeds it checked, and took a move under the rotated basis as a sign that the anti term steers it.

The moves seen here under plain Adam suggest the spike can move without the rotation, so a change of loss alone may be enough to move it.

What we saw. Hardly at all. The mean removal goes from 0.85 against e₁ in the control to 0.87 against the median of the control, and 0.87 against the median of the anchored run. Every run stays within the spill criterion under every baseline.

The one seed that moves by more than a few hundredths is 705, where e₁ was among the busiest axes of the control. There the paired baseline (the gray circle in the figure above) gives a removal about a tenth lower than the medians do. Elsewhere the three baselines agree within a few hundredths, small next to the spread of removal across seeds.

The two medians agree on every seed, within 0.02 of removal. So in these runs the quiet axes of an anchored run cost about what the quiet axes of its control cost, and the anchor hardly touches them.

Spill falls slightly under both medians. On most seeds, e₁ in the control costs a little less on the other ops than the median does, so the paired baseline subtracts less.

The raw spill, with nothing subtracted, is higher (up to 0.038), so some baseline is still needed to separate the anchored op from the general cost of an edit.

The median of eight reference axes drawn at random, as spill-timing takes it, varies with the draw. Over many draws at each seed, the middle nine tenths of the removal spans at most 0.06, about half the seed range of removal, so eight axes are enough to tell a removable run from a weak one.

Over the same draws, spill stays at or below 0.018 on nineteen draws in twenty. The median over all the reference axes needs no draw.

The rotated-basis runs (E3)¶

In the rotated-basis runs, Adam put the cheap directions along the rotated axes. With the median taken along those axes, does the baseline put the short seeds back beside the rest?

What we saw. Yes. Against e₁ in the control, removal falls below half on seeds 706 and 710, and to about six tenths on 707. Again the two medians agree, within 0.04 of removal. The median of eight drawn axes spans at most 0.09 in removal over the middle nine tenths of draws, a little wider than under plain Adam.

The reference axes have to be the cheap ones for the basis the model trained in. Under the rotated basis a coordinate axis is an ordinary direction and costs more, as rotated-basis found, so a median over coordinate axes would overstate the baseline there.

With an optimizer that has no privileged basis at all, every direction touches the spike, and no set of cheap reference directions exists in the same sense.

Two arms of soft-pool, step, soft τ and pool 1–3, soft τ, come close to it at the same seeds: against e₁ in the control, 2 and 4 of their 8 runs spill over it. How do those counts change against the medians? We compare the spill of each run, the largest over the four doses, under each baseline.

What we saw. For step, soft τ the count drops to none: both runs that were over the criterion fall under it against either median. For pool 1–3, soft τ the count barely changes (4 against e₁ in the control, 3 against the median of the control, 3 against the median of the run), but which runs are over it changes. Seeds 708 and 710 fall under the criterion, and seed 705 rises to it: just over against the median of the control, and over by less than a thousandth against the median of the run (the bold 0.020 in the table). The figure shows each run under each baseline and, for scale, with no baseline subtracted.

Which way a run moves depends on the op that spills. On every run over the criterion against e₁ in the control, the e₁ edit costs the spilling op less in the control than the median axis does. So the median subtracts more, and most of the runs that were just over the criterion fall under it. On seed 705 of pool 1–3, soft τ it goes the other way. There e₁ is among the busiest axes of the control (E1), and it costs value-hsv more than the median does, so the paired baseline hides the spill on that op. That fits the spill-timing experiment, which tracked this run against a reference edit and saw its spill grow late on value-hsv, ending near the criterion. The run that spills well over it (seed 711 of pool 1–3, soft τ) stays over under every baseline. So does seed 707, though it was as close to the criterion as 708 and 710. Against the median its largest spill moves to another op, mix, which e₁ in the control costs more than the median axis does.

Removal moves as little as it did for falloff, within a hundredth or two on average.

So the soft-pool spill counts depend on the baseline at the edge: a run within about a hundredth of the criterion can fall on either side. The counts against the median of the run are the ones to compare with later experiments, since the D2.2 design now scores that way. That design takes its median with the syntax axes in, which puts seed 705 just under the criterion, so pool 1–3, soft τ has 2 runs over it on that rule (seeds 707 and 711) and step, soft τ has none.

Discussion¶

For the stored runs of falloff the paired baseline was sound, in that it rarely moved the result. On most seeds e₁ happened to be a quiet axis of the control. Where it was not, the error was a tenth of removal, on one seed. The numbers that split-anti-seeds quoted stand. The paired baseline moved a spillSpill The side effect of an edit on the other ops: the largest drop in the score of any op other than the one removed, net of what the same edit does to a control trained without the anchor. The selectivity criterion of D2.2 allows at most 0.02 at any dose. count only for runs at the edge of the criterion, as in the soft-pool runs of E4.

The median over the quiet axes removes that dependence on the draw, and it takes a few minutes more of scoring per run. Where the draw goes badly, as in the rotated-basis runs, it is the difference between a run that seems to have lost the anchor and one that removes nine tenths of the op.

The agreement of the two medians suggests a baseline needs no control at all: the quiet axes of the anchored run serve. That would matter for a recipe meant for real models, where a matched control is costly or impossible, and it is what spill-timing already does along training.

But the agreement rests on these sixteen pairs, and a recipe that disturbed the quiet axes of the anchored run would break it. So a control is still the way to check that a run-internal baseline is sound.

For later experiments the D2.2 design now takes a simpler scoring rule from this report. Leaving the syntax axes in barely moves the median, so the baseline is the median over every coordinate axis but e₁, with no ranking by energy, taken in the anchored run. An experiment then needs controls only now and then, to confirm that the two medians still agree, rather than one per seed. The one difference to watch is that the median of the run puts spill a little lower than the median of the control (by up to 0.012 here, on one seed). All of this assumes an optimizer like Adam, which keeps most axes quiet. Under one with no privileged basis, any reference direction touches the spike, so the baseline would be larger and an edit of any one direction dearer.
